## The short answer: a Persian grooming schedule

Grooming a Persian is a set of small, regular jobs, not one big event. Most of them you do yourself in a few minutes; a couple are worth handing to a professional.

| Task | How often | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Comb-through | Every day (5 min) | You |
| Eye & face wipe | Daily | You |
| Nail trim + ear clean | Weekly | You |
| Bath | Every 4–6 weeks | You or groomer |
| Full groom / de-mat | Every 1–2 months | Professional |
| Summer comfort-trim | Once, in peak heat | Professional |

The daily comb is the one line that carries the whole table. Get that right and everything else is maintenance; miss it and you end up booking an emergency de-matting instead.

## The daily five-minute comb

The daily comb is the single habit that keeps a Persian out of the clippers. A long coat does not mat because a cat is dirty — it mats because loose undercoat has nowhere to go, and in a day or two it felts into a knot.

Use a stainless-steel wide-tooth comb (a nylon one builds up static and snaps the hair). Work in small sections and comb all the way down to the skin, not just over the surface — surface brushing leaves the knots forming underneath. Spend most of your time on the friction zones where mats always start: behind the ears, under the front legs, the belly, and the "britches" at the back of the thighs. Go gently, keep it short and pleasant, and finish with a treat so the cat looks forward to it. Five calm minutes a day beats a half-hour battle once a week.

## Mats: clip them, never pull them

Once a mat has formed tight against the skin, the honest answer is that it has to be cut out — never pulled. Pulling a mat tears living hair and drags on the skin, and it genuinely hurts; a cat that has been hurt once will fight the comb for months afterwards.

Cutting a dense mat at home is riskier than it looks, too. A cat's skin is thin and loose, so it tents up into the base of the mat — which is exactly how cats get accidentally cut with household scissors. Anything beyond a small surface tangle your fingers can tease apart is a job for a professional with proper clippers. ## Bathing a Persian: every four to six weeks

Persians need bathing more often than most cats, because that long coat holds on to oil, dander and litter dust. Every four to six weeks suits most, but let the coat decide — a greasy, clumpy feel means it is due, a clean silky one means you can wait.

Two rules make a bath go well. First, comb the coat out completely while it is dry; if you wet a tangle it shrinks into a solid mat you will then have to cut. Second, use a cat-formulated shampoo (never human or dog shampoo), keep the water lukewarm and out of the ears, and dry the coat thoroughly with towels and cool air — a damp undercoat left to itself mats as it dries. The same season-and-coat logic we lay out in [how often to bathe a dog in India](/blog/how-often-bathe-dog-india/) applies to cats: more in the sticky pre-monsoon weeks, less in the dry cold.

## Summer comfort-trim: a trim, not a shave

In Ludhiana's April–June heat, when afternoons sit at 40–45°C, a light comfort-trim can genuinely help a heavily coated Persian stay cool and shed fewer mats. A good groomer takes the body coat down to a short, even length while leaving the face, legs and tail fuller — not a bare skin shave.

A full shave is a mistake, not a mercy. The coat gives some insulation against heat and protects thin skin from sunburn, and a shaved Persian coat can grow back patchy or coarser. A comfort-trim is also the kindest fix for a cat that is already badly matted, because clipping a fresh short coat is far gentler than fighting a felted one. Keep your AC-cool indoor cat out of direct afternoon sun either way.

## Why home grooming beats a salon trip for cats

For a cat, the stress usually starts before the grooming does. Cats are deeply territorial, so the carrier, the car and a room full of strange smells and barking dogs can frighten a Persian more than the bath itself. Many cats arrive at a salon already panicked.

Grooming at home keeps the cat on its own ground, where a calm-handling groomer can work in short, patient sessions and stop before the cat tips over into fear. ## When to call a professional

Call a professional the moment the daily comb stops winning. The clear signals: mats you can no longer tease apart with your fingers, matting spreading across the back or belly, a cat that flinches or bites when one spot is touched, red or broken skin under the coat, or flaking and scabs. Those can mean pain, a skin infection, or an underlying health issue rather than just a grooming backlog, so have a vet check anything involving the skin. ## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a Persian cat be groomed?**
Comb it every day for about five minutes, bathe it every four to six weeks, and have a professional full groom or de-mat done every one to two months. The daily comb is the part that actually prevents mats — the bath and the professional groom are maintenance on top of it, not a substitute.

**Meri Persian cat ke baal bahut uljhe hue hain — kya main khud kaat sakti hoon?**
Better not to cut a tight mat at home. A cat's loose skin tents up into the base of the mat, and household scissors catch it very easily, which is how most home grooming cuts happen. Let a groomer clip it out with proper clippers, or book a Full Groom.

**How often should you bathe a Persian cat?**
Roughly every four to six weeks, adjusted to how greasy the coat feels. Always comb the coat out fully while it is dry first — wetting a tangle shrinks it into a mat. Use a cat shampoo, keep water out of the ears, and dry the undercoat thoroughly so it does not mat as it dries.

**Should I shave my Persian cat in summer?**
Not a full shave. A light comfort-trim that leaves the face, legs and tail fuller is fine and can help in the April–June heat, but the coat also protects thin skin from sunburn and gives some insulation. A bare shave can leave a Persian sunburnt and the regrown coat patchy.
