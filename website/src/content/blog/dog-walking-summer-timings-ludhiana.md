## Ludhiana summers: what changes for a walk

April to June is the hard stretch. Mean afternoon highs climb from about 35°C in April to 39°C in May — the hottest month here — with June near 38°C and sticky as the monsoon builds toward July. Three things change for your dog at once: the road turns into a hotplate, dehydration sets in faster, and flat-faced and thick-coated dogs overheat within minutes.

## The 5-second tarmac test

Before you step onto any paved road, press the back of your hand flat on the surface. If you can't hold it comfortably for about **7 seconds**, it's too hot for your dog's paws. Pads are tougher than our skin, but they still burn.

Dark asphalt stores heat long after the air has started to cool: when the air is only 25–30°C, a road in direct sun can reach **50–60°C** — enough to blister a pad in under a minute. On stretches like Ferozepur Road the tarmac bakes all afternoon. Stick to grass, the shaded side of the lane, or carry your dog across short hot patches. Treat the test as a rough, conservative guide — it's strictest for puppies, seniors and thin-padded dogs, which is the point.

## Hot-road paw care

1. Walk on grass or shaded routes; avoid open tarmac and concrete in direct sun.
2. Do the tarmac test before every daytime walk.
3. Keep summer walks short — 10–20 minutes in the peak months.
4. Carry water; offer small sips and wet the paws and belly if your dog looks hot.
5. Check the pads after every walk for redness, blisters or a limp.
6. Consider dog booties or a paw balm for dogs that must cross paved stretches.
7. If a pad looks burned — red, raw or blistered — cool it with **room-temperature (not ice-cold) water for 5–10 minutes** and see a vet. Don't smear on random ointments.

## Heatstroke on a walk: the signs that mean stop now

Heatstroke is the real summer danger, and it builds fast. Most at risk are flat-faced breeds (Pugs, Bulldogs), thick-coated breeds (Labradors, German Shepherds and Golden Retrievers, all common in Ludhiana homes), seniors, puppies and overweight dogs. Watch for:

- Frantic, non-stop panting that won't settle
- Bright red gums or tongue, thick drool
- Vomiting or diarrhoea
- Wobbliness, weakness or collapse
- A body that feels very hot (around 40°C / 104°F or above)

If you see these, **stop the walk.** Get into shade or AC, pour cool — not ice-cold — water over the body, especially the neck, belly and groin, get air moving with a fan, offer small sips only if your dog is alert, and head to the nearest vet straight away. The guidance vets now give is to cool first and travel at the same time. **Ice-cold water and ice packs can do more harm than good.** This is an emergency, not a home-remedy moment — we don't run a 24/7 emergency line, so keep your nearest clinic's number saved.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best time to walk a dog in Ludhiana summers?**
Before about 7:00–7:30am and after 7:00–7:30pm from April to June. Mornings are the safer bet because the roads have had all night to cool; evening tarmac can still be warm well after sunset, so run the tarmac test first.

**Garmi mein kutte ko kab ghumayein?**
Early morning before the sun gets strong, and again after sunset once the roads cool. Through the afternoon, let your dog out only for a quick toilet break in the shade and keep the exercise for the cool hours.

**How do I know if the road is too hot for my dog?**
Press the back of your hand on the surface. If you can't hold it there comfortably for about 7 seconds, it's too hot for paws — move to grass or the shaded side, or carry your dog across the hot stretch.

**My dog still has energy at mid-day — is a short walk okay?**
No. Burn that energy indoors with tug, fetch down a hallway or a food puzzle instead. A mid-day walk on hot tarmac risks burned pads and heatstroke even if your dog seems keen.

**What are the first signs of heatstroke on a walk?**
Heavy, frantic panting that doesn't ease, bright red gums, thick drool, then wobbliness or collapse. Stop, cool your dog with cool (not ice-cold) water, and get to a vet immediately — don't wait to see if it passes.
