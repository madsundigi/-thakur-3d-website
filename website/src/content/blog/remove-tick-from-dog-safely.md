This is general first-aid guidance, not a diagnosis. If your dog seems unwell or the bite looks infected, confirm with your vet.

## The checklist: removing a tick step by step

1. **Get your kit.** Fine-pointed tweezers or a tick-removal hook, plus disposable gloves. Part the fur so you can see the tick on the skin.
2. **Grip at the skin.** Take hold of the tick as close to the skin as possible, by its head — not the fat body.
3. **Pull straight out.** One slow, even motion straight away from the skin. Do not twist, jerk or yank.
4. **Check the bite.** Make sure the head came out. If a piece stays behind and will not lift easily, leave it and call a vet — do not dig.
5. **Clean up.** Wipe the bite with a pet-safe antiseptic, wash your hands, and clean the tweezers.
6. **Kill the tick safely.** Drop it into a sealed bag or a jar of rubbing alcohol. Never crush it between your fingers.
7. **Note the date and spot.** Tick fever can appear one to three weeks later, so you will want the reference.

## Why each step matters

The goal is to lift the tick out whole, without squeezing its body, so it never gets the chance to push germs into the bite.

- **Close to the skin** gives you the mouthparts, not just the body — grab the body and the head can tear off and stay embedded.
- **Straight and steady** keeps those mouthparts intact; twisting or jerking snaps them off under the skin, where they swell and infect.
- **No crushing** matters most. A tick's gut can carry the organisms behind tick fever, so pressing it forces that fluid into the wound and onto your hands.
- **Gloves and clean-up** protect you too — some tick-borne germs can pass to people through a cut or the eyes.

## Home remedies that make a tick bite worse

This is where most of the harm is done. The old tricks share one fault: instead of removing the tick, they stress it while it is still attached, and a stressed tick salivates its gut contents into your dog. Veterinary guidance, including the Merck Veterinary Manual, is consistent — irritants do not make a tick back out safely; they raise the disease risk.

| Remedy | Why it backfires |
|---|---|
| Kerosene | Burns skin, toxic if licked, no quick detach |
| Burning match | Tick salivates germs into the bite; burn risk |
| Crushing it | Pushes infected fluid into the wound |
| Petroleum jelly | Tick feeds longer, then regurgitates germs |

Kerosene is a fuel, not a medicine — it burns the skin, can poison a dog that licks it, and is flammable indoors. A hot match makes the tick regurgitate into the bite and can scorch your dog. Petroleum jelly just lets the tick keep feeding while you wait. None is faster or safer than the tweezer pull above.

## After the tick is out: watch for tick fever

Removing the tick is only half the job; the next three weeks are the other half. Tick fever — the common name for tick-borne infections like ehrlichiosis and babesiosis — usually shows up **one to three weeks after the bite**, not the same day, so a dog can look fine at first. Call a vet promptly if you notice any of these:

- Fever, shivering, or unusual tiredness
- Going off food, or losing weight
- Pale or yellow gums, or dark, cola-coloured urine
- Nosebleeds, bruising, or tiny red spots on the skin
- Swollen glands under the jaw, or limping that moves around

One of these after a tick bite is worth a vet call; two or more is urgent. Our guide to [tick fever in dogs — symptoms and treatment](/blog/tick-fever-dogs-symptoms-treatment/) covers what a vet looks for and how it is treated.

## A note on anti-tick products

Pulling ticks off one by one is first aid, not prevention. The real fix is a vet-chosen monthly product — a spot-on, chewable or collar — matched to your pet. Two honesty points matter here:

- **Dose is species- and weight-specific, and vet-guided.** A product sized for a 30 kg Labrador is wrong for a 5 kg Pomeranian.
- **Never put a dog product on a cat.** Many dog spot-ons contain permethrin, which cats cannot break down; even a small, weight-matched amount can cause tremors and seizures, and there is no antidote. Keep a freshly treated dog away from your cat too.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the safest way to remove a tick from a dog at home?**
Fine-pointed tweezers. Grip the tick as close to the skin as you can, then pull straight out in one slow, steady motion — no twisting. Clean the bite, wash your hands, and seal the tick in a bag rather than crushing it.

**Is kerosene or a burning match a good home remedy for ticks?**
No. Both make the tick salivate or regurgitate into the bite, which raises the chance of passing on disease. Kerosene also burns the skin and is dangerous if your dog licks it. No home remedy beats a steady tweezer pull.

**What if the tick's head stays in my dog's skin?**
Leave it if it will not lift out easily. Digging causes more irritation and infection than the leftover mouthpart, and the skin usually works it out in a few days. If the spot swells, stays red, or oozes, have a vet look at it.

**How soon after a tick bite can tick fever show up?**
Usually one to three weeks later, not the same day. Watch for fever, low energy, loss of appetite or pale gums in that window, and call a vet if you see them — tick-borne illness can move fast once it starts.

**Can I use my dog's anti-tick spot-on on my cat?**
Never. Many dog products contain permethrin, which cats cannot process and which can cause tremors and seizures even in small, weight-matched doses. Use only a cat-labelled product your vet approves.
